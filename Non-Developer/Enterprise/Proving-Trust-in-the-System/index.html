AI Governance - Proving Trust in the System

Analyze, limit, and govern AI use with documented controls, evidence, and clear accountability

Welcome to "Proving Trust in the System." Over the next hour, we will tackle one of the most important topics in enterprise AI adoption: governance. Part 1

Why AI Governance Matters

Before we explore the tools and controls, let's establish why governance matters. The regulatory environment is shifting, and your obligations depend on where you operate, the data involved, and how you deploy AI. A documented decision is more defensible than assuming that a vendor feature settles those questions.

The Regulatory Landscape

  • The EU AI Act has risk-based obligations and phased application dates; assess your role and use case
  • US federal and state AI policy continues to evolve; assign an owner to monitor applicable changes
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides voluntary guidance, not a compliance certification
  • Existing obligations such as HIPAA, SOX, or PCI-DSS may intersect with AI use, depending on scope
  • Work with legal and compliance teams to document applicable obligations, controls, and evidence

Sources: EU AI Act application dates · NIST AI RMF. Verified 2026-10-27.

Your first governance decision is about applicability, not a product toggle. The EU AI Act takes effect in stages, and obligations differ by role and type of use. Using a coding assistant does not by itself establish that your organization operates a high-risk AI system. In the US, assign someone to track relevant federal, state, and sector developments rather than relying on an old executive-order summary. NIST's framework can structure your risk assessment, but it is voluntary. HIPAA, SOX, and PCI-DSS address different obligations; they are not interchangeable AI-specific mandates. Your legal and compliance teams should decide which requirements apply and what evidence is needed.

Organizational Risk Without Governance

Intellectual Property Exposure

Without clear controls, proprietary code or trade secrets could be sent to unapproved AI services, creating data-handling and IP risks.

Compliance Gaps

Ungoverned AI tools create shadow IT scenarios. Missing evidence makes it harder to demonstrate that required controls operate effectively.

Reputational Damage

An incident involving unreviewed AI-generated code, a vulnerability, or a license dispute can erode customer trust.

Uncontrolled Adoption

Developers may adopt personal AI tools when governed alternatives are unavailable, creating risks outside approved enterprise controls.

Let's talk about what happens when governance is absent. Proprietary source code may reach services whose terms and retention your organization has not assessed. Invisible usage makes it harder to demonstrate control. Unreviewed generated code can introduce security or licensing problems, just as other unreviewed code can. And when approved tools are too difficult to access, people may choose personal alternatives with different protections. Governance is not about promising zero risk. It is about making the risks, controls, and remaining gaps visible enough to manage.
Part 2

The GitHub Copilot Trust Center

Now that we have established why governance matters, let's look at GitHub's trust resources. They provide commitments and supporting documents to evaluate. We still need to check which product, agreement, feature, and reporting period each document covers.

Data Handling and Privacy

  • Training: Business and Enterprise customer inputs and outputs are not used to train generative AI models without customer authorization
  • Retention: No training does not mean no storage; service history, session records, and provider processing have separate rules
  • Security evidence: Review current encryption specifications and the scope and period of SOC 2 Type II reports; an attestation is not a guarantee for every feature
  • Residency: GitHub Enterprise Cloud with data residency supports US/EU inference restrictions when the applicable policy is enabled; regional model and client requirements apply

Sources: Customer terms · Model hosting · Residency requirements.

The most common question is whether GitHub trains models on your code. For Business and Enterprise customer data, the documented commitment is no training without customer authorization. That is different from promising that nothing is stored. Some features retain inputs and outputs to provide the service, and session history may exist locally or in the cloud. Model-provider agreements and preview features can also have different processing conditions. For encryption and assurance, obtain the current technical specifications and actual attestation scope rather than relying on a badge or an unverified protocol number. Residency is now available for supported US and EU deployments of GitHub Enterprise Cloud with data residency, but the policy must be enabled and compatible clients and regional models must be used. Purchasing Copilot Enterprise alone does not establish that configuration.

Part 3

Content Exclusions

Data-handling terms describe how information is processed. Content exclusions help restrict which files inform supported Copilot experiences. They are useful preventive controls, but they are not a universal data-loss-prevention boundary.

How Content Exclusions Work

  • Repository administrators and organization owners can configure repository or path exclusions within their applicable scope
  • Clients retrieve the applicable policy; supported suggestions, chat responses, and reviews respect exclusions
  • IDE Edit and Agent modes are not covered; indirect semantic context, symlinks, and remote filesystems have limitations
  • Loaded IDE settings can take up to 30 minutes to refresh; supported IDEs provide reload or restart options
  • CLI coverage is unverified: current official concept and configuration pages disagree; confirm before relying on it

Sources: Scope and limitations · Configuration and refresh. Verified 2026-10-27.

An exclusion tells supported Copilot experiences not to use specified content. The client retrieves policy from GitHub, so it is misleading to describe this as an unbypassable server-side boundary. IDE Edit and Agent modes are not covered, and indirect information such as type definitions can still reach context. Symlinks and remote filesystems also have limitations. A changed rule can take up to 30 minutes to reach an IDE with settings already loaded; a supported reload or restart can refresh earlier. The official concept table and configuration guide currently disagree on CLI support, so we cannot treat CLI exclusions as verified. Before approving a sensitive workflow, test the exact client and mode and use separate access controls or disable unsupported surfaces where necessary.

Content Exclusion Strategy

Common Exclusion Targets

  • Repositories containing trade secrets or proprietary algorithms
  • Sensitive configuration files; remove and rotate exposed credentials separately
  • Content subject to legal or regulatory restrictions, as determined by counsel
  • Third-party vendor code with contractual or licensing restrictions

Best Practices

  • Maintain an exclusion registry reviewed quarterly
  • Align exclusions with data classification labels
  • Document rationale, owner, supported clients, and test evidence
  • Review new repositories, policy changes, and unsupported modes
Take a risk-based approach rather than assuming either that every repository must be excluded or that an exclusion resolves every risk. Review trade secrets, regulated data, legal restrictions, and vendor obligations with the relevant owners. If credentials are committed, exclusions are not a substitute for removing and rotating them. For each rule, record why it exists, who owns it, and which clients and modes were tested after the refresh interval. Review the registry quarterly and when repositories or policies change. If a sensitive workflow uses an unsupported mode, decide whether to disable that surface or apply additional controls instead of claiming the exclusion protects it.
Part 4

Audit Logging

Preventive controls need detective evidence, but no single log proves the entire governance picture. Let's separate changes to access and settings from usage metrics and the contents of individual sessions.

Copilot Audit Log Events

  • Seat and policy events: Review supported license assignments, removals, and settings changes
  • Content exclusions: Review recorded changes to exclusion settings
  • Hosted agent activity: Review supported GitHub-hosted agent events separately from administrative events
  • Evidence boundary: Audit logs do not automatically include local prompts or all client session activity
  • Retention: Audit events are retained for 180 days; Git events for seven days; export for your required retention period

Source: Copilot audit-log coverage.

These records help answer who changed a setting or received access, and supported agent events add evidence about work on GitHub. They are not a transcript of every Copilot interaction. Local prompts and client session data do not automatically appear in the enterprise audit log. Team-based assignment events can also require care when interpreting the actor. For long-term evidence, assign an owner to export and retain the relevant records. GitHub documents 180 days for audit events and seven days for Git events, so the default product history may be shorter than your organization's retention requirement.

Integrating Audit Logs with Your SIEM

Collection Options

  • Enterprise audit streaming to supported destinations such as Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage, Azure Event Hubs, Google Cloud Storage, or Splunk
  • REST API for supported custom queries and integrations
  • Customer-owned retention, restricted access, delivery checks, and duplicate handling

What to Monitor

  • Policy changes outside approved change windows
  • Unapproved license assignments or exclusion removals
  • Supported hosted-agent events and collection failures
  • Usage trends from metrics, not assumed prompt-level audit coverage

Sources: Streaming destinations and delivery · REST audit API.

Audit evidence becomes useful when it reaches the team responsible for acting on it. The listed destinations remain supported, but destination support does not mean collection is configured or complete. Confirm delivery health, access restrictions, retention, and duplicate handling; streaming uses at-least-once delivery. Alert on changes that do not match approved tickets, and investigate unexpected license or exclusion changes. If you want to monitor adoption or interaction spikes, use an appropriate metrics source and understand its client coverage. A usage trend is not the same as a complete record of prompts, and an audit log is not automatically tamper-proof evidence.
Part 5

IP Indemnity & Code Provenance

One of the top concerns for legal teams is intellectual property. Where do suggestions come from, and what happens if one matches public code? We will separate suggestion-matching evidence from contractual defense, because neither is a blanket originality guarantee.

Understanding Code Provenance

  • Copilot generates suggestions from model behavior; outputs can still match public code
  • Block matching suggestions: The public-code filter checks for matches in supported products
  • Allow matching suggestions: Supported experiences can display public-code references and available license information
  • References concern Copilot suggestions, not a comprehensive scan of existing project code or a record of exact training sources
  • Missing references or a filter result do not prove originality; retain human, licensing, and security review

Source: Finding matching public code.

A model can generate text rather than retrieve a file and still produce a suggestion that resembles public code. The matching filter and references help you manage that possibility. When matching suggestions are blocked in a supported product, detected matches are not shown. When they are allowed, supported experiences can show references to similar public code, with license information where available. A reference can also have no asserted license. This is not a general scanner for everything already in your repository and does not reconstruct the model's training history. Use it as one input to your license and code-review process, not proof that all unflagged code is original.

IP Indemnity

What the Agreement Provides

For direct GitHub purchases, current generative AI terms extend third-party claim defense to outputs if the governing Agreement provides that defense. Microsoft purchases follow Microsoft terms. Coverage, conditions, and limitations require legal review.

What This Means for You

Document the purchasing channel, applicable terms, claim scope, and customer duties. Duplicate detection is not a required mitigation for GitHub offerings under the current Microsoft copyright commitment, effective April 3, 2026; it remains an optional control.

Sources: GitHub terms, March 2026 · Microsoft GitHub terms · Current required mitigations.

Contractual defense can mitigate risk, but it is not automatic coverage for every claim simply because a customer bought a paid plan. GitHub's current direct-purchase terms say defense applies if the governing Agreement provides it. Purchases through Microsoft follow Microsoft's terms, and earlier agreements or renewals may need separate review. Your legal team should identify the actual covered claims, limitations, and customer responsibilities. One important current detail is that Microsoft's GitHub-specific required mitigations no longer require duplicate detection for copyright commitment coverage as of April 3, 2026. We should not reintroduce an outdated filter prerequisite, but we also should not translate that change into a promise of unlimited coverage. Document the contract, not just the product name.
Part 6

Copilot Policies

Content exclusions are preventive controls within their supported scope, while audit logging provides detective evidence. Copilot policies add preventive decisions about which features, agents, and models people can use. The key is to understand where each policy is enforced.

Organization-Level Policy Controls

  • Public-code matching: Allow or block matching suggestions; references are supported behavior, not a third policy state
  • Features and clients: Govern available Chat, IDE, CLI, code-review, and cloud-agent capabilities using the applicable policies
  • Models and external tools: Approve model access and MCP/tool integrations with their own permissions and terms
  • Managed settings: Centrally constrain supported client behavior; verify supported keys, versions, and enforcement
  • Access and change control: Assign licenses through supported controls and review defaults for new features and models

Sources: Organization policies · Enterprise-managed settings.

The public-code matching choice is Allow or Block, not Block with reference. More broadly, current governance extends beyond Chat and completions to agents, clients, models, and external tools. Availability and enforcement choices depend on the policy and your enterprise settings. Managed settings can constrain supported clients, but each key has its own coverage and version requirements. Likewise, a Copilot MCP policy is not blanket control over every third-party application or the permissions of an external service. Review both feature access and the external tool's authority. Finally, decide how unconfigured new features and models should become available, so a rollout does not silently bypass your approval process.

Enterprise Policy Inheritance

Enterprise Level

  • Explicitly enable or disable supported policies, or delegate decisions to organizations
  • Enforced enablement and disablement can both prevent organization overrides
  • Review default availability and enterprise-assigned-user settings

Organization Level

  • Choose settings where the enterprise delegates control
  • Apply stricter settings only where the applicable policy permits
  • Check multi-license conflicts; the strictest organization setting does not always win

Sources: Policy scope and conflicts · Default availability.

Policy inheritance is not simply a ladder where every organization can always become stricter. If the enterprise explicitly enables a feature everywhere, an organization may be unable to disable it. If the enterprise disables it, the organization cannot turn it on. Local choices depend on delegation and the particular policy. Users licensed through multiple organizations also need attention: within the same enterprise, the least restrictive setting often applies, with exceptions. Users receiving licenses directly from the enterprise have additional policy handling. For a sensitive team, test the effective policy for a representative user instead of assuming the setting on one organization proves the outcome.

Responsible AI Frameworks

We have covered individual controls. Now let's connect them to broader responsible AI principles. A useful mapping explains how controls support a principle and what additional evidence is still needed; it does not certify that the principle has been satisfied.

Microsoft's Responsible AI Principles

  • Fairness: AI systems should treat people equitably
  • Reliability & Safety: AI systems should perform reliably and safely
  • Privacy & Security: AI systems should protect data and resist attacks
  • Inclusiveness: AI systems should empower and engage everyone
  • Transparency: People should understand capabilities and limitations
  • Accountability: People remain accountable for AI systems and their use

GitHub describes following Microsoft's six responsible AI principles. These are goals and responsibilities, not guarantees that a feature automatically produces a fair or safe result. A policy guardrail may support a control environment, but fairness still needs evaluation. Accessibility needs evidence rather than an assumption that all users are included. Transparency includes acknowledging data and audit limitations, not just collecting logs. Accountability means that someone owns the decision and the residual risk. This is the evidence distinction to carry into your governance documentation. Audit events help explain administrative changes and supported hosted-agent activity. Metrics describe trends, but client telemetry can undercount and is not a direct measure of productivity. Session records can reveal detailed work, yet access and sharing depend on where the session ran and how it was stored. Contract terms establish commitments, while a test shows whether a particular control operated in your environment. For each row, record an owner, collection scope, retention period, and known gaps. That makes the mapping useful without overstating what it proves.
Part 8

Building a Governance Playbook

You now understand the individual controls and their evidence boundaries. The next step is assembling them into a governance playbook that your organization can execute, maintain, and improve over time.

Your Governance Playbook

  • Policy documentation: Version-control approved users, data, clients, modes, models, and external tools
  • Training requirements: Require responsible-use training and track completion before granting access
  • Monitoring cadence: Assign owners for audit collection, exclusion tests, policy reviews, and evidence retention
  • Privacy decisions: Approve session storage, syncing, sharing, and access; review preview and third-party terms
  • Incident response: Define escalation for data exposure, IP claims, policy violations, and collection failures
  • Continuous improvement: Test changes, review developer feedback, and reassess residual risks

Sources: Session storage and access · Approval resources.

A playbook is a living operational document. Specify the approved workflows, train users, and keep the evidence owners visible. For session privacy, distinguish local records, synced personal sessions, and hosted-agent records. Enabling syncing does not automatically give administrators access to a user's personal session data; hosted-agent sessions have a different sharing model. Approve retention and sharing deliberately, and review the terms for previews and third-party tools instead of assuming general product coverage applies everywhere. Establish a monitoring cadence that suits your risk, with additional tests after changes. Finally, define how people report a suspected exposure or collection failure, and use feedback to improve controls without driving usage into ungoverned alternatives.

Reporting to Regulators and Boards

Evidence for Applicable Requirements

  • Policies, owners, and dated control-test results
  • Audit coverage, retention, and known collection gaps
  • Use-case risk assessments and residual-risk decisions
  • Vendor terms, attestation scope, and legal review

Decision-Ready Board Reporting

  • Approved AI tools, risk posture, and control effectiveness
  • Adoption trends and separately measured business outcomes
  • Incident counts with definitions and reporting coverage
  • Named owners, gaps, and prioritized improvement roadmap

Metric limitations: Client attribution gaps · Customization metrics and coverage.

A credible report tells the audience both what you know and what you cannot establish. For an applicable regulatory review, connect the requirement to a control, its owner, and dated evidence. For the board, separate adoption from measured outcomes. A higher interaction count is not proof of productivity, and zero recorded incidents is not proof that nothing happened. GitHub's October 2026 notices describe client-attribution gaps and telemetry limitations; record supported clients and coverage when interpreting trends. Named customization metrics can help identify tool adoption, but configured names may themselves be sensitive. A useful summary might say that a control was tested on specified clients, which exceptions remain, who owns them, and when they will be reviewed.
Summary

Wrap-Up & Next Steps

Let's bring everything together and identify the actions you can take starting today.

Key Takeaways

  • Governance combines applicable obligations, approved workflows, accountable owners, and evidence
  • Content exclusions and policy controls have specific scope, client support, and refresh requirements
  • No training does not mean no retention; verify session, provider, and residency conditions separately
  • Audit events, usage metrics, and session records answer different questions and have different gaps
  • Public-code references support review; contractual defense depends on the governing terms
  • A living playbook and transparent reporting support responsible adoption without claiming zero risk
The central lesson is to match each assurance to evidence. Content exclusions help prevent certain context use, but unsupported modes and propagation matter. Business and Enterprise no-training commitments do not promise zero service retention. Audit records, metrics, and sessions each answer different questions. Matching-code references support human review rather than certify originality, and legal defense depends on the actual agreement. Your playbook brings these pieces together through owners, training, tests, monitoring, and incident response. Report the remaining gaps openly so stakeholders can make an informed decision about risk.
