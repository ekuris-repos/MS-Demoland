Governing AI Spend: Budgets, Tokens, and Cost Optimization

AI usage, governance, and spend management

Welcome to our session on managing AI usage as an operational and financial capability. We are going to connect the mechanics of tokens and AI credits to the decisions budget owners actually make: who gets access, where spend belongs, what signals matter, and when a guardrail should stop work.

The Question Behind the Dashboard

Are we converting AI consumption into measurable, governed productivity?

Too little control

Unexpected spend, unclear ownership, and weak forecasting.

Too much control

Blocked work, hidden demand, and lower return on licensed tools.

Cost control is not the same as cost minimization. A perfectly flat usage line can mean disciplined operations, but it can also mean nobody is using the capability. Our goal is to create enough visibility and accountability to support productive usage without allowing variable cost to become an unmanaged surprise.

Part 1

Usage-Based Billing Fundamentals

We will begin with the unit economics. Once the relationship among prompts, tokens, models, credits, and dollars is clear, the budget controls become much easier to reason about.

The Billing Model Changed

  • GitHub Copilot usage-based billing became generally available on June 1, 2026
  • Copilot Business and Enterprise usage is measured in GitHub AI credits
  • Usage now reflects the model selected and tokens consumed
  • Paid usage is enabled by default for organizations and enterprises
  • Existing customer promotional included amounts run through September 1, 2026
The shift matters because a request is no longer treated as a uniform unit. Two interactions can create very different costs depending on the model, the context supplied, and the work performed. As of August 2026, existing customers are still in the introductory period, so current consumption may not predict post-promotion overage without adjustment.

What Is an AI Credit?

1 GitHub AI credit = $0.01 USD

  • AI credits translate model-specific token charges into one billing unit
  • A lightweight interaction may consume a fraction of a credit
  • A long agent session across many files may consume substantially more
  • A $10 paid-usage budget represents 1,000 AI credits
The one-cent conversion gives finance and engineering a common language. Tokens explain the technical consumption, credits normalize different model rates, and dollars connect the result to a budget. We should still inspect token detail because the same credit total can arise from very different behaviors.

Four Token Categories Matter

CategoryWhat it representsTypical driver
InputContent sent to the modelPrompt, files, repository context
OutputContent generated by the modelAnswer length and generated code
Cache readPreviously cached context reusedRepeated stable context
Cache writeContext written for later reuseLarge reusable context
Input and output are the easiest categories to visualize, but the current AI usage report also exposes cache reads and writes. That detail is valuable because it helps explain why repeated workflows can have a different cost profile from a new session, even when the visible prompt looks similar.

From Interaction to Invoice

graph LR
  A[User task] --> B[Context]
  B --> C[Model]
  C --> D[Token categories]
  D --> E[Model rates]
  E --> F[AI credits]
  F --> G[Included or paid]
  G --> H[Net amount]
There are several transformations between a user action and an invoice. That is why raw prompt counts are weak cost measures. We need to know the model and token volume before we know credit consumption, then know whether those credits came from the included pool or paid usage before we know the billed amount.

The Cost Relationship

AI credits = sum of token category quantities multiplied by the model's published rates

Volume

More context and output generally increase consumption.

Rate

Different models price token categories differently.

We do not need everyone to calculate a model invoice by hand. We do need leaders to understand that model choice and token volume are independent levers. A smaller prompt sent to a high-cost model and a large prompt sent to a lower-cost model can produce similar credit totals for different reasons.

What Consumes AI Credits?

Consumes credits

Copilot Chat, Copilot CLI, Copilot cloud agent, Copilot Spaces, Spark, and third-party coding agents.

Included in paid plans

Code completions and next edit suggestions remain unlimited and do not consume AI credits.

A metered feature is not automatically a bad choice. Agentic work can create enough value to justify substantial consumption. The practical policy is to use the least expensive surface that reliably completes the task, while preserving higher-capability tools for work that benefits from them.

Included Credits Form a Shared Pool

PlanStandard monthly credits per seatEnterprise behavior
Copilot Business1,900Pooled at the billing entity
Copilot Enterprise3,900Pooled at the billing entity

Example: 100 Business seats create a shared pool of 190,000 AI credits.

Pooling recognizes that productive usage is uneven. One user may run complex agentic workflows while another relies mainly on completions. The organization does not have to transfer credits manually between them. Governance should preserve this flexibility while preventing a small group from consuming the pool without visibility.

Pool Lifecycle Rules

  • The pool resets at 00:00 UTC on the first day of each calendar month
  • Unused included credits do not carry over
  • Adding licenses increases the pool immediately
  • Removing licenses reduces the pool at the next billing cycle
  • After exhaustion, usage is blocked or becomes paid overage according to policy and budgets
Calendar-month reset behavior matters for forecasting. License invoicing and credit refresh timing can feel like one process, but included usage resets on a fixed UTC boundary. Teams should align recurring reports to that boundary so partial periods are not mistaken for complete monthly trends.

A Simple Planning Example

This is deliberately simple. It establishes the bridge from seats to included capacity, then from excess credits to dollars. A production forecast should segment users and include uncertainty, but every stakeholder should be able to follow this base calculation before discussing a more sophisticated model.

Three Common Misreads

MisreadCorrect interpretation
Credits equal billed dollarsIncluded credits may reduce net amount to zero
A request has one fixed priceToken volume and model determine credit use
A budget is always a hard capBlocking depends on budget type and configuration
These distinctions prevent most billing conversations from going off course. Consumption is not the same as billed cost, a user action is not a uniform billing unit, and a budget threshold can be informational rather than restrictive. We should always name which layer and which measure we mean.

Part 2

Budget Layers and Governance

We now have the consumption model. The next step is to place controls around it. GitHub provides several controls that work at different phases, so choosing the right one requires more precision than simply setting a dollar amount.

The Control Map

User-level budget

Caps: individual total consumption
Active: pool and paid phases
Stop: always

Included usage control

Caps: cost-center share of pool
Active: pool phase
Stop: configurable outcome

Cost center or organization

Caps: scoped paid usage
Active: after pool exhaustion
Stop: optional

Enterprise limit

Caps: enterprise paid usage
Active: after pool exhaustion
Stop: optional

The most important column is when the control becomes active. User-level budgets are unique because they govern both included and paid credits. The broader dollar budgets generally control additional charges only after the shared pool is exhausted.

User-Level Budgets Govern Total Consumption

  • Apply during both the shared-pool phase and paid-usage phase
  • Always enforce a hard stop
  • A $0 budget blocks AI-credit-consuming features immediately
  • Universal budgets provide a default for all licensed users
  • Users appear in the budget list after they first consume credits in a cycle
This is the strongest fairness control and the easiest one to misuse. A universal user limit can prevent a few users from consuming the pool, but it can also block legitimate power users. Inactive licensed users will not appear in the universal budget list for that cycle, so the licensing page remains the complete population view.

User Budget Precedence

graph TD
  A[Universal per-user default] --> B[Cost center per-user override]
  B --> C[Individual override]
  C --> D[Most specific setting wins]
Precedence allows a broad policy with controlled exceptions. Engineering can have a different per-user default from marketing, and an individual working on a major migration can receive a higher limit. The individual setting wins over the cost-center setting, which wins over the universal default.

Included Usage Controls Protect a Cost Center's Share

  • Limit a cost center to credits funded by licenses assigned to it
  • Operate before the enterprise pool is exhausted
  • GitHub calculates the cap from the cost center's license mix
  • The cap adjusts as licensed membership changes
  • At the cap, members can be blocked or continue into paid overage
This control changes the pooling behavior for a cost center. It is useful when departments require protected allocations, but it reduces the flexibility of one large shared pool. That tradeoff should be explicit: stronger internal allocation means less natural balancing between light and heavy users across the enterprise.

A Cost Center Has Two Different Levers

Included usage control

Limits how much of the shared pool the cost center can draw before metering.

Cost center budget

Limits the cost center's paid charges after the shared pool is exhausted.

These controls sound similar because both mention cost centers, but they operate in different phases. One allocates included capacity. The other governs paid overage. A team may use either control or both, depending on whether the concern is fair access to the pool, additional charges, or both.

Organization Budgets Follow License Attribution

  • Cap metered charges for users whose Copilot seat is billed to the organization
  • Become active after the shared pool is exhausted
  • Are the only Copilot budget option available to organization owners
  • Can restrict below enterprise policy but cannot override a higher-level limit
  • Multiple organization licenses can make attribution less predictable
Organization scope is practical when organizations map cleanly to financial ownership. It becomes harder to interpret when a user receives Copilot licenses from multiple organizations, because GitHub selects one billing organization for the cycle. Direct cost-center assignment is more predictable when internal chargeback must be stable.

The Enterprise Limit Is an Overage Limit

Total maximum = fixed license fees + enterprise paid-usage limit

  • It does not cap total monthly GitHub Copilot cost
  • It applies after the included pool is exhausted
  • Blocking must be explicitly enabled if a firm ceiling is required
  • Cost centers can be excluded when they have independent spending authority
This is a critical finance distinction. A five-thousand-dollar enterprise limit does not mean the total Copilot bill cannot exceed five thousand dollars. License charges remain fixed, and the limit applies to additional usage. A cost-center exclusion can preserve an independently funded team's work even when the broader enterprise limit is reached.

How a Request Passes Through Controls

graph LR
  A[Request] --> B{User limit?}
  B -->|Available| C{Pool available?}
  B -->|Exhausted| X[Blocked]
  C -->|Yes| D[Use included credits]
  C -->|No| E{Paid usage enabled?}
  E -->|No| X
  E -->|Yes| F{Applicable paid budget?}
  F -->|Available| G[Metered charge]
  F -->|Blocking limit reached| X
The user limit is checked first. If it blocks the request, a larger enterprise or cost-center budget cannot rescue it. If the user has headroom, GitHub checks the pool, then the paid-usage policy and applicable overage budget. In practice, the control with the least remaining headroom stops the user first.

Overlapping Controls Create Surprise

  • Multiple applicable budgets can count the same paid usage
  • Any exhausted blocking budget can stop the workflow
  • Raising an enterprise limit does not unblock an exhausted user limit
  • Raising user limits without enough paid headroom can shift blockage upstream
  • Simple, documented control sets are easier to forecast and support
More controls do not automatically produce better governance. Every overlapping limit adds another possible explanation for a blocked user. We should be able to identify the governing control quickly and explain why it exists. If two controls have no distinct purpose, one of them is probably unnecessary.

Cost Allocation Depends on the Product

Billing patternCost-center attribution
Usage-based products such as ActionsRepository or organization where usage occurs
License-based products such as Copilot seatsUser assigned to the cost center
AI-credit productsUser, or licensing organization if no direct assignment exists
Cost centers are not a universal tag applied the same way to every product. Repository activity drives some costs, while user assignment drives licenses and AI credits. A chargeback model should follow GitHub's allocation rules instead of assuming that one organizational hierarchy explains every SKU.

Design Cost Centers Around Financial Decisions

Strong boundaries

Business units, funded programs, regulated environments, and approved experiments.

Weak boundaries

Every small team, temporary project, or reporting label with no budget owner.

Separate cost centers are required when teams need separate budgets.

A cost center is useful when someone owns the forecast and can act on the result. Creating one for every organizational wrinkle produces administrative overhead without improving decisions. GitHub supports up to one thousand active cost centers per enterprise, but the practical limit should be the number your operating process can govern well.

A Balanced Starting Configuration

LayerStarting postureReason
Paid-usage policyEnabled with a controlled limitAvoid abrupt loss of capability
EnterpriseAlerts plus reviewed overage ceilingProtect aggregate exposure
Cost centerOnly where ownership is clearSupport allocation and exceptions
UserUniversal default plus named exceptionsPreserve fairness and power users
This posture gives the organization a ceiling without treating every user identically. The universal limit establishes a baseline, while cost-center and individual overrides support different roles. We should size these amounts from observed consumption and business demand, then revisit them on a defined cadence.

Part 3

Usage Visibility and Reporting

Visibility is where policy becomes testable. GitHub now separates product summaries, general metered usage, and AI-specific analysis. We will use each view for the question it answers best.

Three Views, Three Jobs

ViewBest questionTypical audience
Billing overviewWhat products and licenses are we consuming?Owners and billing managers
Metered usageWhere is paid product usage and cost occurring?FinOps and platform teams
AI usageWho and which models are consuming AI credits?Enterprise AI governance
The reporting experience has become more specialized. The overview is a product-level entry point. Metered usage supports cross-product cost exploration. AI usage adds the model, token, and user detail required to manage Copilot under the new credit model.

Billing Overview: Start Broad

  • Open billing from personal, organization, or enterprise settings
  • Use product tabs to review consumed usage and licenses
  • Open details when a product requires deeper analysis
  • Available data varies by account role and plan
The overview is the right starting point for a periodic account review because it prevents AI spend from being discussed in isolation from licenses and other metered products. It is not the final view for root-cause analysis, but it tells us where deeper investigation is warranted.

Metered Usage: Explore Cross-Product Cost

  • Filter usage directly on the page
  • Group results according to the selected filters
  • Choose the time frame for the chart and breakdown table
  • Inspect quantity, gross amount, discount amount, and net amount
  • Download chart data or request broader usage reports
Metered usage is where we distinguish activity from billable cost. A high gross amount with a matching discount amount may be fully covered by included usage. Net amount is the actual charge, while quantity and discount explain how we arrived there.

AI Usage: Analyze Credit Consumption

  • Review total AI credits across the enterprise
  • Compare included-pool consumption with additional paid usage
  • Identify high-consumption users and models
  • Assess adoption across licensed organizations
  • Download chart data or request a detailed AI usage report
This is the central operational view for Copilot usage-based billing. It helps us separate a broad adoption increase from a concentrated model or user pattern. The right follow-up is not automatic restriction. It is a conversation about the work behind the consumption and whether the budget structure still fits.

Role Determines What You Can See

RoleRelevant visibility
Enterprise owner or billing managerEnterprise usage and direct user filtering in AI usage
Organization ownerOrganization usage, without direct user filtering in the AI view
Organization owner needing user detailDownload an applicable usage report
IndividualPersonal usage unless the license is assigned by an organization or enterprise
A missing filter may be a permissions issue rather than missing data. Enterprise owners and billing managers can filter AI usage by user. Organization owners cannot do that directly in the view, but can obtain per-user detail through a report. We should design the reporting process around the roles that actually hold these permissions.

Choose the Right Download

ReportMaximum periodUseful detail
Summarized usageOne yearDate, SKU, organization, repository, cost center
Detailed usage31 daysAdds username and workflow path

The report period determines the level of detail available. Long-range summarized data is useful for finance trends. Thirty-one-day detailed data supports operational investigation. Reports are delivered by email, the link expires after twenty-four hours, and only one report per account can be requested at a time.

Fields That Explain the Bill

FieldManagement meaning
quantityHow much of the SKU was consumed
gross_amountCost before included usage and discounts
discount_amountAmount covered by inclusion or discount
net_amountActual billable amount
model and token fieldsWhy AI credit consumption occurred
A good report preserves both consumption and financial treatment. Gross minus discount equals net. For AI, model and token fields explain the underlying behavior. If we retain only net amount, we lose the ability to understand growing demand that is currently covered by included credits.

The API is the right path when billing data must feed a repeatable finance or BI workflow. The usage summary endpoint is aggregated. Detailed web reports contain fields such as username and workflow path that the general REST usage endpoint does not provide, so the automation design should account for that difference.

This also allows internal business context to sit beside GitHub consumption data without changing the source of truth.

Part 4

Predictability Without Sacrificing Productivity

We have the mechanics, controls, and evidence. The final section combines them into an operating model. Predictability comes from feedback and explicit choices, not from finding one perfect limit and leaving it unchanged.

Use Four Levers Together

Entitlements

Match licenses and included capacity to the population that can use them.

Guardrails

Set user and overage controls according to risk tolerance.

Guidance

Teach users to choose appropriate surfaces, models, and context.

Evidence

Review adoption, consumption, cost, and outcomes together.

Budgets alone are reactive. Entitlements set the population, guardrails bound exposure, guidance changes daily behavior, and evidence tells us whether to adjust. Weakness in any one lever creates pressure on the others. For example, poor guidance often leads administrators to compensate with restrictive limits.

A Weekly Operating Cadence

SignalQuestionPossible action
Pool burn rateWill included credits last through month end?Forecast paid usage
ConcentrationWhich users, models, or units changed?Validate use case
Blocked usageIs policy stopping valuable work?Adjust exception or limit
Net amountIs overage within approved tolerance?Escalate or continue
Outcome evidenceWhat value did high usage produce?Expand, coach, or retire
Weekly review is frequent enough to catch a changing pattern without turning every interaction into an approval event. The review should connect anomalies to work. A spike caused by an approved migration has a different response from one caused by broad, repetitive context or an abandoned experiment.

Recommendations by Maturity

StageRecommended focusAvoid
StartingBaseline usage, define owners, enable alertsArbitrary tight limits
ScalingCost centers, user defaults, recurring reportsOne dashboard for every audience
OptimizingSegment forecasts, exceptions, outcome measuresEquating low spend with success
Controls should mature with evidence. A new program needs a baseline more than a complex hierarchy. A scaling program needs allocation and standard exceptions. An optimizing program can connect usage patterns to value and forecast by cohort. Skipping directly to advanced controls usually creates false precision.

Your First 30 Days

  1. Confirm paid-usage policy, budget owners, and alert recipients
  2. Inventory licenses, cost-center assignments, and existing budgets
  3. Capture a full calendar month of AI usage and net cost
  4. Identify high-value power users and unexplained outliers
  5. Set or revise universal, cost-center, and individual limits
  6. Publish a monthly leader scorecard and weekly admin review
The first month should produce an operating baseline, not a final policy. Confirm who can make each decision, collect one complete cycle, and then tune limits around actual cohorts and approved use cases. The scorecard should show what changed, why it matters, and which action is being taken.
